# Chapter 3 summary — Lexing: regular languages and automata

The reader now sees the lexer as the front end's first phase. It turns characters into tokens, each with a token kind and a lexeme, and drops whitespace and comments so the parser sees only meaningful units. Cooper and Torczon (2nd ed.) justify the split as a separation of lexical from syntactic structure, not mainly speed. Two rules choose among token rules: maximal munch (Go spec: "the longest sequence of characters that form a valid token") and priority for equal-length ties, such as keyword over identifier. C's `x+++++y` lexes as `x ++ ++ + y` and gcc rejects it ("lvalue required as increment operand"). Go's semicolon insertion is a lexer rule that looks at the line's last token, and `go/scanner` shows it with literal `"\n"`.

He knows regular expressions (concatenation, alternation, Kleene star), NFAs with ε-transitions, Thompson's construction (linear size, O(mn) set simulation), and Cox's 2007 backtracking timings. In the lab, Python `re` took 244.9 ms against 0.25 ms for the NFA at n=24. He knows ε-closure, subset construction, the 2^n worst case (the n-th-from-last family), and minimization (Hopcroft O(n log n), Brzozowski, a unique minimal DFA). The `automata` lab built 21 NFA states, 9 DFA states and 8 after merging. Its DFA state 8 holds the IF/IDENT tie, and priority picks IF. Longest-match scanning backs up to the last accepting position and can go quadratic (`a` / `a*b`). flex uses longest match then first rule, and warns "rule cannot be matched". re2c emits direct-coded DFAs. rustc_lexer is hand-written. Go hashes identifiers into a keyword map. The book's Go lexer has positions and ERROR tokens.
